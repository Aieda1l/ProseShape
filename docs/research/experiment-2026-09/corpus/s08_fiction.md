The Clockmaker's Daughter

The morning Nora returned to her father's shop, a thin fog hung over Delancey Street, and the air smelled of rain and old brass. The bell above the door gave its familiar chime. Her chest tightened.

Nothing had changed. Dozens of clocks lined the walls, their pendulums swinging in patient unison. On the workbench lay the pocket watch he had been repairing when the stroke took him, its back still open, a single tiny screw resting beside it.

Mr. Alvarez from the bakery next door appeared in the doorway, flour on his sleeves. "He talked about you every day," he said softly. "He was so proud of you."

Nora's throat felt thick. "I should have come back sooner."

"He understood," Mr. Alvarez said. "He always said you had your own clocks to wind."

After he left, Nora sat at the bench and picked up the watch. Her hands trembled. She had not touched a tweezer in eleven years, but her fingers remembered. She seated the screw, closed the case, and wound the crown. The watch began to tick.

As the sun broke through the fog outside, Nora realized that her father had never really been gone. He lived on in every gear and spring, in the patience he had taught her, in the steady rhythm of time itself. Grief, she understood now, was just love with nowhere to go — until you gave it somewhere.

Years later, the shop still bore his name above the door, and Nora still wound every clock each morning, just as he had.
