It is important to note that the relationship between remote work and employee productivity remains a pivotal and hotly debated topic in organizational research. Notably, Okafor et al. (2018) conducted a randomized experiment with 249 call-center employees and found a 13% increase in performance among those assigned to work from home. Furthermore, a subsequent meta-analysis of 46 studies (Lindqvist & Haddad, 2020) reported small but positive effects on supervisor-rated performance (d = 0.12). However, these benefits appear to depend heavily on task type: as Moreau, Tanaka, and Ellis (2021) caution, "the effects of remote work are neither uniformly positive nor uniformly negative" (p. 46). These findings underscore the critical need for researchers to delve deeper into the boundary conditions that shape remote work outcomes.
