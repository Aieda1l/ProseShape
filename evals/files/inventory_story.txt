The Inventory

The Harlan County Historical Society occupied the second floor of the old bank building on Main Street, above a nail salon and a shuttered pharmacy. On the Monday June Petrakis learned it would close, the late-autumn light came through the tall windows in pale slants, catching the dust above the filing cabinets.

June was forty-one, with graying hair she kept pinned up and reading glasses she was always losing. She had worked at the Society for eleven years, the last six as its only paid employee.

"The county's pulling the funding at the end of the month," said Frank Delaney, the board treasurer, turning his hat in his hands. "I'm sorry, June. They want an inventory before everything goes to the state archive."

She had two weeks.

She started with the founder's papers. Everett Harlan had established the Society in 1931, and his portrait still hung by the door: stern, mustached, a man certain of his place in history. His collection was orderly, labeled in his precise hand.

On the fourth day, at the back of a cabinet no one had opened in years, she found a hatbox tied with twine. Inside were sixty-three letters, all addressed to Everett, all signed Adela Moss.

June read them at her desk until the streetlights came on. Adela had been a schoolteacher in the county's eastern hills. She had interviewed families, copied parish records, traced the old mill roads on foot. She sent Everett her findings, page after page, and asked, politely and then less politely, when her name would appear alongside his.

It never did. June went back to Everett's published history of the county, the one that sat in every school library. Whole paragraphs matched Adela's letters word for word.

The next morning she called Frank.

"The final exhibit," she said. "I want to change it. I want it to be about Adela Moss."

Frank was quiet on the line. "The Harlan family still gives money to half the town, June."

"The Society is closing anyway," she said. "What's the point of preserving history if we only preserve the parts that are comfortable? Adela did the work. She deserves to be remembered for it."

After a long pause, Frank sighed. "All right. It's your exhibit."

June spent the next ten days building it. She mounted the letters in acid-free sleeves beside the matching pages of Everett's book. She found a single photograph of Adela in a 1928 school yearbook: a thin woman in a dark dress, squinting into the sun. She wrote the captions herself, carefully, without anger.

The exhibit opened on the Society's last Friday. More people came than June expected: teachers, a few students, an old man who said his grandmother had been in Adela's class. A reporter from the county paper took photographs. Even Frank came, hat in hand, and stood a long time in front of the yearbook picture.

That night, alone in the emptied office, June stood at the window. She realized that she had spent eleven years caring for other people's stories without ever asking whose stories were missing. Adela had waited almost a century to be seen. June had finally seen her, and in doing so, she had found her own purpose again.

Outside, the first snow of the year began to fall, soft and clean, covering the town in white.

A year later, the Harlan County Historical Society reopened in a new space, funded by a state humanities grant awarded on the strength of the Adela Moss exhibit. Its new name was painted in gold on the door: the Moss-Harlan Historical Society. June still worked there. Every morning she passed Adela's photograph, now hanging where Everett's portrait used to be, and every morning she nodded to her, one keeper of stories to another.
