The morning of the last crossing, fog clung to the water like a held breath. Tomas arrived at the dock at five, as he had every day for twenty-two years. The air smelled of diesel and salt and the faint sweetness of rotting kelp. Gulls cried overhead. Somewhere in the distance, the new bridge loomed, its steel cables glinting in the pale light.

His chest tightened as he looked at it.

He ran through the pre-departure checklist with practiced hands: fuel, radio, life jackets, the bilge pump that had been temperamental since spring. Each item was a small ritual, a thread connecting him to the man he had been when he first took the wheel.

The passengers trickled in. Mrs. Adeyemi, who had crossed every Thursday to visit her sister. The Kowalski twins, now grown, who had once pressed their faces to the window to watch for seals. A young reporter from the county paper with a camera around her neck.

"How does it feel?" the reporter asked. "Knowing this is the end?"

Tomas considered the question. "The bridge will get people across faster," he said. "But a ferry isn't just a way across. It's a place where people wait together. You can't build that out of steel."

She wrote that down.

The crossing took forty minutes. Halfway over, the fog began to lift, and sunlight spilled across the water in long golden bands. Mrs. Adeyemi squeezed his arm as she disembarked. His throat felt thick.

After the last passenger left, Tomas sat alone in the wheelhouse. He realized that he had been afraid of this day not because he would lose his job, but because he would lose the version of himself that the ferry had made. And he understood, finally, that the man would remain even if the boat did not. The crossings were part of him now.

Years later, when people asked him about the ferry, he would tell them about the fog, and the seals, and the way the light came through on that last morning. And he would smile.
